How to Play

Movement

  • W / S or ↑ / ↓ — Move forward & back
  • ← / → — Slide left & right
  • Mouse — Look around
  • G — Toggle compass

Investigating

  • E — Enter rooms & use elevators
  • F — Pick up evidence when the hint appears
  • Golden orbs mark evidence locations — approach to trigger the hint
  • Open Bull's Notebook to review clues, suspects, and your notes

Making an Accusation

  • Collect at least 15 clues before you can accuse
  • Open Bull's Notebook → Accuse tab
  • Name the suspect, the room, and the weapon
  • Wrong accusations are penalised — be sure before you commit

Scoring

Scoring can be toggled on or off in Bull's Notebook → Notes tab.

  • Start with 1,000 points
  • −1 point per 10 seconds elapsed
  • −200 points per wrong accusation
  • +50 points for each clue under the 15 minimum when you solve
  • −25 points for each clue over the 15 minimum when you solve

The Detective's Handbook

The Three Pillars of Every Case

Every murder investigation rests on three questions. Your suspect must satisfy all three.

  • Motive — Why would they want the victim dead? Look for jealousy, greed, revenge, or fear.
  • Means — Did they have access to the weapon or the physical ability to commit the act?
  • Opportunity — Could they have been in the right place at the right time? An alibi eliminates opportunity.

Reading Witness Statements

Witnesses lie, misremember, and omit. Ask yourself:

  • Does this person have a reason to protect someone?
  • Does their account match the physical evidence?
  • Are they certain of the time, or guessing?
  • Were they alone, or can their presence be corroborated?

Establishing a Timeline

The time of death is your anchor. Work outward from it. Where was each person one hour before? During? After? Gaps in a suspect's timeline are as telling as contradictions.

A strong alibi places someone far from the crime room at the exact time of death — with a witness or document to prove it.

Physical Evidence

Objects don't lie — but they can mislead. A weapon found in a room proves it was there, not who put it there. Ask:

  • Who had access to this location?
  • Could this evidence have been planted?
  • Does it point to the same suspect as the witness testimony?

Cross-Referencing Clues

A single clue is a lead. Two clues pointing the same direction is a pattern. Three is a case. Never accuse on one piece of evidence alone — look for convergence across witness accounts, documents, and physical finds.

When You're Ready to Accuse

Open Bull's Notebook and go to the Accuse tab. You'll need to name the suspect, the room, and the weapon. You must have gathered enough evidence first. Choose carefully — wrong accusations are noted.
